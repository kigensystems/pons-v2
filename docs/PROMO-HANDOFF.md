## Copy-and-paste creative prompt

You are helping art-direct and produce a short brand film for **Plum**, a software/infrastructure project with a nostalgic visual identity. Develop the concept, shot ideas, generation prompts, edit, sound, and ending from this brief. Preserve its ambiguity and personality.

The central idea is **a strange little film about the world Plum connects, with the computer somewhere inside it.** Imagine **late-night television in 1994, caught between channels**: ordinary places, obsolete machines, small human moments. Slightly disconnected images gradually make us feel that something is happening underneath them.

The focus is Plum's infrastructure and the world it connects. Express that through things receiving, responding, passing something onward, and quietly continuing to work. Use these as visual metaphors; do not invent technical features, performance claims, or product capabilities.

The Macintosh is incidental set dressing. It can sit in the corner of an office or briefly appear on a cluttered desk. It does not need to appear at all in an individual shot or test. Do not turn the film into a Macintosh reveal, an Apple/iMac commercial, a tour of hardware, or a sequence of glamour shots of keys and bezels. The earlier concept made this mistake and the user explicitly rejected it.

### The feeling

Mysterious, retro, nostalgic, hazy, dreamy, slightly unsettling, then familiar. The feeling of a commercial you half-remember seeing late at night. Something ordinary feels a little strange; by the end, that strangeness becomes comforting. The world seems to have been quietly running all this time.

Keep the unease gentle and unresolved for a while. No jump scare or horror climax. Nostalgia should come from the places, light, objects, sounds, and editing. It should feel lived-in, slightly imperfect, and emotionally recognizable.

### References and identity

Get familiar with Plum's opening, Explore, and About, and with **https://www.shader.se/**. Shader inspired the site. Take its optical softness, luminous serif typography, grain, highlight bloom, spatial haze, and uncanny old corporate-brochure feeling as atmosphere references. Retain Plum's own identity and develop original scenes.

Plum's visual vocabulary includes forest-green darkness, warm ivory plastic, cream paper, glowing Apple Garamond, and the supplied rainbow plum mark. The film can also use washed-out daylight, muted blue evenings, tobacco browns, faded domestic colors, and occasional rainbow accents. Every shot does not need an identical green grade.

Think early-1990s film photography transferred to analog television, with some moments that feel like local broadcasts or family tapes. Gentle diffusion, organic grain, softened highlights, restrained color bleed, modest tape softness, imperfect focus, and slightly awkward framing. Keep materials and physical light believable. Avoid polishing everything into a pristine contemporary commercial or drowning everything in fake VHS damage.

### Structure and images

The commercial can feel **a little messy and unrelated**. Use an associative montage. It does not require a literal plot, a narrator explaining the product, or an obvious reason for every cut. Sound, recurring light, and rhythm can suggest the connections.

Possible fragments include:

- An empty swimming pool at dusk, with a telephone ringing somewhere out of view.
- An answering machine in a dark kitchen, its red message lamp blinking.
- Someone pulling a cable through an opening in a wall.
- A grainy weather broadcast that holds a little too long.
- Headlights crossing the ceiling of an empty office.
- A printer beginning to feed paper when nobody is around.
- A sun-faded home-video fragment: someone turns toward the camera, and we cut before they speak.
- Apartment windows lighting up one after another.
- A railway signal changing.
- A brief glimpse of a Macintosh on a cluttered desk, half outside the frame.

These are a pool of ideas, not a mandatory checklist or locked storyboard. Offer better fragments if they fit. Allow ordinary human life to sit beside the systems that quietly support it. Avoid turning every shot into a close-up of an electronic device.

Use unexpectedly long holds, abrupt cuts, and sounds that continue into a different scene. Let the montage be strange without becoming random stock footage: a repeated sound, a light responding elsewhere, or a recurring gesture can give it a subtle underlying rhythm. Prefer actual editorial cuts to AI morphing transitions. Leave room for curiosity.

### Sound

Sound gives the apparent mess its shape. Build with telephone tones, room hum, a mechanical relay, printer noise, distant television, outdoor ambience, and a few warm synth notes. The sound can arrive before its source or continue after the picture changes.

A small original musical phrase keeps trying to emerge through the noise. When Plum appears, we finally hear it clearly. The resolution should feel reassuring and familiar, without becoming a triumphant technology-launch crescendo. Use original or appropriately licensed audio; do not borrow a recognizable commercial recording or computer startup sound. Voiceover is optional, not a requirement. The first tests used no dialogue.

### Required ending

After the montage, let the image drop out or settle. Reveal **the supplied logo and Plum**. Give that identity a clear moment. Then reveal **a familiar feeling.** Let the sound resolve and linger.

Use the existing logo and wordmark treatment. No logo redesign. Composite the exact mark and typography in the edit so the result stays faithful and readable. Do not make the entire film an extended logo reveal or rely on a video model to redraw the brand correctly.

### Production approach

Generate individual shots or small montage studies, then find the rhythm in the edit. Direct each shot with its setting, visible event, camera behavior, lighting, texture, and sound. Keep enough freedom for an interesting result. Use still or video references when a particular composition or continuity matters; an incidental prop does not require the same elaborate reference workflow as a hero product.

Use text-to-video to explore these different places. Test whether each model understands the mood, cuts, mundane details, and sound. A short multi-shot test is useful, but the finished film does not have to be generated in one pass. Keep final pacing, sound bridges, grading, texture, and exact branding under editorial control.

Do not lock the finished film to the ten-second test length or the previously proposed 15/24-second durations. Duration and delivery format remain open. X/social was mentioned in the earlier project brief; the later destination question was not answered. The comparative tests were 16:9. A 4:3 broadcast composition remains an option, not a settled requirement.

The models discussed and tested through OpenArt are **Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash**, **MiniMax H3 Max by fal**, and **Wan 3.0**. No winner has been chosen. Review their actual output against this brief. Hardware fidelity alone is not the deciding criterion for this direction. Favor emotional fit, believable period detail, interesting restraint, usable cuts and motion, sound, and revision effort. Recheck availability and cost before any newly requested batch.

Avoid: Macintosh-centered storytelling, product-demo pacing, generic futuristic server rooms, holographic networks, neon cyberpunk, glossy technology-launch imagery, forced crypto symbolism, heavy glitch transitions, an unrelated AI-showreel feeling, and promotional claims we have not established. Keep the film curious, human, odd, and quietly connected.

The next useful step is to review the existing three tests, identify which moments feel right, and propose a small set of stronger shots or a rough edit. Distinguish suggestions from decisions and completed generations from visually approved work.
